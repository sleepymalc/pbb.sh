**TL;DR.** **Dr. Post-Training** recasts general training data as a *data-induced regularizer*: at each step the scarce target batch names the ideal update direction, the abundant general batch induces a feasible set $U_t$ of admissible directions, and the update is the Euclidean projection of the target gradient onto $U_t$. Standard training, target-only fine-tuning, and existing data-selection methods are special cases with different feasible sets, ordered on a bias–variance spectrum. Two quantities of $U_t$ govern everything: a **bias** (how far the set is from the population target gradient) and an **exposed variance** (how much target noise the set lets through). They bound the error of a single update and decide whether the trajectory converges on the target. **Group-Wise Subset Update** turns the regularization strength into an explicit knob, and a one-pass, memory-tight system realizes it at a per-step cost on par with standard training. Across SFT, RLHF, and RLVR the best strength tracks the mismatch between general and target data: gains are largest where the target is diluted in the pool (F1 on *less-mix/tydiqa* **4.9% → 48.3%**), and standard training stays competitive where the two are aligned.

Large language model (LLM) post-training operates on *scarce, high-fidelity target data* alongside abundant but imperfectly aligned general data. Training on the target alone is faithful but statistically noisy; training on the general pool is stable but biased by the misalignment. Existing work asks *which* general samples to keep; we ask *what constraint* they should impose on the update. The answer is a single projection step that subsumes standard training and prior data-selection methods, exposes a richer design space — **Group-Wise Subset Update** — and comes with a theory that says when each point on the spectrum should win.

## Why Dr. Post-Training

- **[One unified framework](#method).** A single projection-based update that subsumes standard training, target-only fine-tuning, and existing data-selection methods (top-$k$, thresholding, greedy curation) as special cases on one bias–variance spectrum — across SFT, RLHF, and RLVR.
- **[Two quantities govern the spectrum](#bias-variance-tradeoffs-and-convergence).** The *bias* of a feasible set and the *exposed variance* it lets through bound the one-step error via a noisy projection oracle inequality, and the same two quantities decide whether repeated updates converge on the target objective.
- **[Group-Wise Subset Update](#our-proposal-group-wise-subset-update).** Strictly enlarges the feasible set over Global Subset, so its bias is provably no larger, while its exposed-variance bound grows at most $P$-fold in the number of groups.
- **[Gains track the mismatch](#results).** On the broad *less-mix* pool, Layer-Wise Subset lifts *tydiqa* F1 from **4.9% → 48.3%**; on *Tulu 3*, IFEval rises **32.5% → 35.3%**; in RLHF the curated updates reach standard PPO's final reward about **5× sooner** and end an **order of magnitude lower in toxicity**; in RLVR on MATH, final accuracy goes **63.0% → 63.8%**. Where the pool is already in-distribution, standard training and Global Subset are competitive or best — exactly as the theory predicts.
- **[Plays well with the modern stack](#system-a-one-pass-memory-tight-implementation).** LoRA, [MeSO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03507), activation checkpointing, and gradient accumulation — no two-pass backward, no retained graph.
- **[Per-step cost on par with standard training](#system-efficiency-on-par-with-standard-training-at-3b).** The curated step costs **+20%** on SmolLM2-360M, **+8%** on TinyLlama-1.1B, and **−2%** on Llama-3.2-3B, where skipping half of the weight gradient pays for the scoring.

## Method

### Problem setup

At step $t$ the optimizer draws two batches: a training batch $B_t = \{z_i\}_{i=1}^n \sim \mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and a (small) target batch $B_t^\star = \{z_j^\star\}_{j=1}^m \sim \mathbb{P}_\star$. Write the per-sample gradients $g_i, g_j^\star$ and their batch means $\hat g_{\mathrm{tr}}, \hat g_\star$. Standard post-training updates $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta_t u_t$, and our central modeling choice is to pick

$$
u_t \;=\; \arg\min_{u \in U_t}\, \mathcal{L}_\star(\theta_t - \eta_t u),
$$

where the *feasible set* $U_t$ is built from the training batch $B_t$ only, and the target batch $B_t^\star$ enters solely through the target loss $\mathcal{L}_\star$. *The target batch identifies the desired direction; the training batch constrains which directions are admissible.*

### From constrained optimization to a projection

Under a standard $\beta$-smoothness assumption on $\mathcal{L}_\star$, majorization–minimization on the quadratic upper bound and plugging in the target-batch estimate $\hat g_\star$ for the (unknown) population gradient $g_\star$ reduces this constrained optimization to a Euclidean projection:

$$
u_t \;=\; \arg\min_{u \in U_t}\, \lVert u - \hat g_\star \rVert^2 \;=\; \operatorname{Proj}_{U_t}(\hat g_\star).
$$

The geometry of $U_t$ is now the only design choice — and it is the *data regularizer*.

### Existing methods as special cases

Three familiar training paradigms drop out of the framework by choosing different feasible sets:

- ***Target-Only*** $U_t^\star = \mathbb{R}^d$. No constraint; $u_t = \hat g_\star$ recovers gradient descent on the target batch — unbiased but high-variance when $m$ is small.
- ***Full-Training*** $U_t^{\mathrm{tr}} = \{\hat g_{\mathrm{tr}}\}$. Singleton; $u_t = \hat g_{\mathrm{tr}}$ recovers standard SFT/RLHF — zero variance but a distribution-mismatch bias floor $\lVert g_{\mathrm{tr}} - g_\star \rVert^2$.
- ***Global Subset*** $U_t^{\mathrm{glob}}(k) = \{g_S : S \subseteq [n], \lvert S \rvert = k\}$. All $k$-sample averages of training gradients. Existing data-selection methods (Top-$k$, thresholding, GREATS-style greedy) are tractable approximations to the resulting subset optimization, since

  $$
  \min_{S} \lVert g_S - \hat g_\star \rVert^2 \;\equiv\; \min_S\, \lVert g_S \rVert^2 - \frac{2}{k}\sum_{i \in S} \langle g_i, \hat g_\star \rangle,
  $$

  which trades an *alignment* term $s_i := \langle g_i, \hat g_\star \rangle$ against a *redundancy* penalty.

### Our proposal: Group-Wise Subset Update

A single global subset $S$ shared across *all* parameters is overly restrictive: a sample useful for one layer may be useless for another. **Group-Wise Subset Update** relaxes this by partitioning parameters into groups $\mathcal{G} = \{G_p\}_{p=1}^P$ and letting each group pick its own subset:

$$
U_t^{\mathrm{grp}}(k; \mathcal{G}) = \prod_{p=1}^P \left\{ \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in S} g_i^{(p)} : \lvert S \rvert = k \right\}.
$$

Because the projection factorizes, each group solves an independent top-$k$ on its own score $s_i^{(p)} := \langle g_i^{(p)}, \hat g_\star^{(p)} \rangle$. The practically important case is **Layer-Wise Subset Update**, with one group per linear layer.

The chain $U_t^{\mathrm{glob}}(k) \subseteq U_t^{\mathrm{grp}}(k;\mathcal{G}) \subseteq U_t^\star = \mathbb{R}^d$ orders all four families by expressiveness, and both subset families collapse to $U_t^{\mathrm{tr}}$ at $k = n$. The partition $\mathcal{G}$ and the subset size $k$ are the explicit knobs that move along the regularization spectrum: coarser partitions regularize harder, finer ones let the target drive more of the update.

### Bias-variance tradeoffs and convergence

  <img src="/research/Dr-Post-Training/Figures/spectrum.png" alt="Data-regularization spectrum: Target-Only, Group-Wise Subset, Global Subset, and Full-Training arranged from weakest to strongest regularization, trading variance for bias." />
  <figcaption><strong>Data-regularization spectrum.</strong> Four feasible sets, ordered by how much they constrain the update. Moving right (stronger data regularization) reduces variance at the cost of bias; moving left does the reverse. The two subset families occupy the middle, and the partition $\mathcal{G}$ and subset size $k$ slide along the axis.</figcaption>
</figure>

We measure the quality of an update $u$ at step $t$ by its **mean-squared error** against the unknown population target gradient $g_\star = g_\star(\theta_t)$,

$$
\mathrm{MSE}(u) \;\coloneqq\; \mathbb{E}\bigl[\lVert u - g_\star \rVert^2 \,\bigm|\, \theta_t\bigr],
$$

with the expectation over both batches. **Why bound MSE?** Because majorization–minimization turns it directly into per-step progress on the target loss:

<div class="lemma">

**Lemma** (Descent). *Under a $\beta$-smooth target loss with $\eta_t \leq 1/\beta$,*

$$
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathcal{L}_\star(\theta_{t+1}) \,\bigm|\, \theta_t\bigr] \;\leq\; \mathcal{L}_\star(\theta_t) - \frac{\eta_t}{2}\lVert g_\star \rVert^2 + \frac{\eta_t}{2}\,\mathrm{MSE}(u_t).
$$

</div>

The projected update $u_t = \operatorname{Proj}_{U_t}(\hat g_\star)$ can miss $g_\star$ for two reasons. **Bias:** restricted to training-supported directions, $U_t$ may contain nothing near $g_\star$. **Variance:** the projection sees $\hat g_\star = g_\star + \xi$, so the target-batch noise $\xi$ perturbs which element of $U_t$ it picks.

<div class="remark">

**Intuition** (Only the noise along candidate differences matters). The choice between two candidates $u, u' \in U_t$ depends on $\xi$ only through $\langle u - u', \xi \rangle$. Noise orthogonal to every candidate difference cannot change the projection, so a feasible set is exposed to exactly the noise living in its *update-difference subspace*.

</div>

Formally, for a feasible set $U_t$ define the **bias** $\mathcal{B}(U_t) \coloneqq \mathbb{E}\bigl[\inf_{u \in U_t} \lVert u - g_\star \rVert^2\bigr]$ and the **exposed variance** $\mathcal{V}(U_t) \coloneqq \mathbb{E}\bigl[\lVert \operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{D}(U_t)}(\xi) \rVert^2\bigr]$, where $\mathcal{D}(U_t) \coloneqq \operatorname{span}\{u - u' : u, u' \in U_t\}$. The bias is a property of the regularizer alone and does not depend on $m$; the exposed variance equals $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{D}(U_t)} \Sigma_\star)]/m$ and shrinks with the target batch.

<figure class="figure-block figure-right-sm">
  <img src="/research/Dr-Post-Training/Figures/Theory/one-step-error.png" alt="Geometry of the one-step error: a feasible set U_t drawn as a blob, the population target gradient g-star outside it, the noisy estimate g-hat-star displaced by noise xi, and the projection u_t on the set. The distance from g-star to the set is the bias; the gap between MSE and bias is the excess error." />
  <figcaption><strong>Geometry of the one-step error.</strong> The bias is the distance from $g_\star$ to $U_t$; the noise $\xi$ moves the projection to $u_t$, and the gap between $\mathrm{MSE}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ is the excess error.</figcaption>
</figure>

<div class="theorem">

**Theorem** (One-step error, informal). *For any feasible set $U_t$ determined by the training batch, the projected update satisfies*

$$
\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}(u_t)} \;\leq\; \sqrt{\mathcal{B}(U_t)} + 2\sqrt{\mathcal{V}(U_t)} .
$$

*If $U_t \subseteq U_t'$, then $\mathcal{B}(U_t') \leq \mathcal{B}(U_t)$ and $\mathcal{V}(U_t) \leq \mathcal{V}(U_t')$: enlarging the feasible set can only decrease the bias and increase the exposed variance.*

</div>

The proof compares $u_t$ with the oracle choice $u^\dagger \in \arg\min_{u \in U_t} \lVert u - g_\star \rVert$ only through the minimality of $u_t$, so it holds for the discrete, non-convex feasible sets above, where the projection is neither unique nor Lipschitz. For target noise with variance at most $\sigma^2/m$ per direction, the two quantities are:

<div class="table-scroll">

| | Full-Training | Global Subset | Group-Wise Subset | Target-Only |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | ← strong: $\mathcal{B}\uparrow$ $\mathcal{V}\downarrow$ — *data regularization* — weak: $\mathcal{B}\downarrow$ $\mathcal{V}\uparrow$ → | | | |
| $\mathcal{B}(U_t)$ | $\lVert g_{\mathrm{tr}} - g_\star \rVert^2 + \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{tr}})/n$ | $\mathcal{B}(U_t^{\mathrm{glob}}(k))$ | $\leq \mathcal{B}(U_t^{\mathrm{glob}}(k))$ | $0$ |
| $\mathcal{V}(U_t)$ | $0$ | $\leq (n-1)\sigma^2/m$ | $\leq P(n-1)\sigma^2/m$ | $\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_\star)/m \leq d\sigma^2/m$ |

</div>

Three patterns govern the spectrum:

- **The two extremes are mirror images.** Full-Training pays the full mismatch but sees no target noise; Target-Only is unbiased but exposed to all $d$ noise directions. The training-batch term $\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{tr}})/n$ counts as *bias* because it persists even with a noiseless target.
- **Subset methods expose only $n-1$ directions.** Any two $k$-subset averages differ by a combination of the $n-1$ gradient differences $g_i - g_1$, so the exposed variance of Global Subset is at most $(n-1)\sigma^2/m$ — it depends on the feasible set only through the dimension of its update-difference subspace, not on $k$ or on the number $\binom{n}{k}$ of candidates.
- **Refining the partition lowers bias and grows the variance bound at most $P$-fold.** Because $U_t^{\mathrm{glob}}(k) \subseteq U_t^{\mathrm{grp}}(k;\mathcal{G})$, the group-wise bias is no larger; the update-difference subspace splits into one block per group, so the bound becomes $\sigma^2 \sum_p \min\{\lvert G_p \rvert, n-1\}/m$.

Squaring the bound gives $\mathrm{MSE}(u_t) \leq \mathcal{B}(U_t) + 4\sqrt{\mathcal{B}(U_t)\mathcal{V}(U_t)} + 4\mathcal{V}(U_t)$. Since $\mathcal{V}(U_t) = O(1/m)$ while the bias does not depend on $m$, the excess error $\mathrm{MSE}(u_t) - \mathcal{B}(U_t)$ decays as $1/m$ when $\mathcal{B}(U_t) = 0$ but only as $1/\sqrt m$ otherwise — the rate set by the cross term, and the worst case over near-ties between candidates.

<div class="remark">

**Remark** (Two complexity measures). The classical finite-class argument bounds the same excess error through the *cardinality* of the feasible set under a gradient-norm bound $\lVert g_i \rVert \leq C$: $\mathcal{E}(u_t^{\mathrm{glob}}) \leq \tfrac{4C\sigma}{\sqrt m}\sqrt{2\log\bigl(2\binom{n}{k}\bigr)}$, and $P$ times that for Group-Wise. Neither bound dominates: the finite-class bound has no cross term but needs $C$ and decays as $1/\sqrt m$ even when the bias vanishes; the oracle inequality needs no norm bound and keeps the $1/m$ rate in that case. A unified bound lets every group pay the *smaller* of the two measures, $\min\{\dim \mathcal{D}(U_t^{(p)}),\, 2\log\binom{n}{k} + 2\}$ — for our $n = 8$, $k = 4$ the dimension wins (7 versus about 10.5), whereas large candidate batches with small subsets favor the log-cardinality.

</div>

Which update dominates depends on the target batch size, the mismatch, and the feasible-set complexity. Under *small* mismatch all four methods have a regime: as $m$ grows the preferred update moves from Full-Training to Global Subset to Group-Wise Subset to Target-Only. Under *moderate* mismatch Group-Wise dominates Global over a wider range of $m$. Under *large* mismatch the bias of Full-Training and Global Subset is large enough that Group-Wise wins even at small $m$, before Target-Only takes over once enough target data are available. The SFT pools below span exactly these regimes: the in-distribution *Dolci* pools, the mixed pool, and *Tulu 3*, where the target skill is most diluted.

**From one step to the trajectory.** Summing the descent lemma over steps bounds the average target gradient by the average one-step error, so convergence hinges on the error *relative to the current gradient*. We say the feasible sets *cover* the target gradient along the trajectory if

$$
\sqrt{\mathcal{B}(U_t)} + 2\sqrt{\mathcal{V}(U_t)} \;\leq\; q\,\lVert g_\star(\theta_t) \rVert + \phi_t \quad \text{for every } t, \text{ with } q < 1,
$$

that is, some feasible direction approximates the target gradient to within a fraction $q$ of its length while exposing comparable noise, up to a floor $\phi_t \geq 0$ from the training–target mismatch and the target noise.

<div class="theorem">

**Theorem** (Convergence on the target, informal). *Let the feasible sets cover the target gradient with $q < 1$, let $0 < \eta_t \leq 1/\beta$, and $T \geq 1$. Then*

$$
\frac{\sum_{t < T} \eta_t\, \mathbb{E}\lVert g_\star(\theta_t) \rVert^2}{\sum_{t < T} \eta_t}
\;\leq\; \frac{4}{1 - q^2} \cdot \frac{\mathcal{L}_\star(\theta_0) - \inf_\theta \mathcal{L}_\star(\theta)}{\sum_{t < T} \eta_t}
\;+\; \frac{2(1 + q^2)}{(1 - q^2)^2} \cdot \frac{\sum_{t < T} \eta_t\, \phi_t^2}{\sum_{t < T} \eta_t}.
$$

*If the floors vanish and the step size is constant, then $g_\star(\theta_t) \to 0$ almost surely; under the Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition the target loss converges linearly and $\theta_t$ converges almost surely, with finite path length, to a minimizer of $\mathcal{L}_\star$.*

</div>

The first term is the optimization term — initial suboptimality over the total step size — and vanishes as $\sum_t \eta_t \to \infty$. The second is a *stationarity floor* set by the part of the one-step error that does not shrink with the gradient: distribution mismatch and target noise. No convexity of $\mathcal{L}_\star$ or of $U_t$, and no uniqueness of the minimizer, is required. The theorem also makes the knob quantitative. Refining the partition improves the coverage ratio (the bias part of the condition) but enlarges the exposed dimension $d_{\mathrm{eff}} = \sum_p \min\{\lvert G_p \rvert, n-1\}$ (the variance part); in the relative-noise regime the condition $q < 1$ reads $m > 4 d_{\mathrm{eff}}\sigma_1^2/(1 - \sqrt{\rho})^2$, so a finer partition is *certified* only when the coverage it gains outweighs the noise it exposes — and since exposed noise scales as $1/m$, **larger target batches certify finer partitions**.

<div class="takeaway">

**Takeaway.** *Larger feasible sets reduce bias but raise exposed variance*: the update gains freedom to align with $g_\star$ but **also with the noise** $\xi$. The same two quantities govern convergence along the trajectory. Full-Training is stable but biased, Target-Only unbiased but unstable at small $m$, and the subset updates interpolate between them — which is best depends on the mismatch, and the experiments below follow that prediction.

</div>

### Beyond two distributions

The framework needs only two ingredients: a **training signal** that can be optimized reliably but may be biased, and a **target signal** that is too noisy or costly to optimize directly. In SFT the target batch provides the noisy signal and the training batch induces the feasible set. Reinforcement learning has the same structure: the signal of interest is the *expected reward*, but optimizing it directly by policy gradient is high-variance, whereas surrogates such as PPO's clipped objective or GRPO's group-relative estimator stabilize training at the cost of optimization bias. Treating the reward gradient as the target and the surrogate's per-rollout gradients as the feasible set gives the RLHF and RLVR instantiations below. The convergence guarantees are stated for the two-distribution setting; the structure motivates the RL variants without extending the guarantees to them.

## System: a one-pass, memory-tight implementation

Two naive implementations make data-regularized updates impractical at LLM scale: a *two-pass* approach (run backward once for scoring, again for the assembled update — doubles runtime), and the *retain-graph* approach (`retain_graph=True` in PyTorch — keeps the whole autograd graph alive, blowing memory). We instead carefully manage the lifetime of a small number of intermediate tensors so the full data-regularized update fits in a *single* forward–backward pass with peak memory close to standard training.

### Standard training: what gets kept where

Solid arrows are explicit dependencies; dashed arrows cross the forward/backward boundary. <em>Bottom rows:</em> the lifetime (purple/orange bars) of the four key tensors — input activation $a^{(l)}$, pre-activation $e^{(l)}$, and the two backward signals $\partial \ell / \partial e^{(l)}$ and $\partial \ell / \partial a^{(l)}$. Each tensor is allocated and released as soon as its last consumer finishes, so the per-layer memory footprint stays roughly constant across the entire backward pass.</figcaption>
</figure>

Crucially, standard backprop *never materializes* the per-sample weight gradient $g_i^{(l)}$; it just folds it into the batch average. Our method needs $g_i^{(l)}$ twice — once for scoring, once for assembling the selected subset — which is exactly the dependency that breaks the standard schedule.

### Customized tensor lifetime scheduling

The key idea is a *swap*: at each layer, replace the forward-cached pre-activation $e^{(l)}$ with the backward-generated gradient $\partial \ell / \partial e^{(l)}$, which has the same shape and is no longer needed for upstream computation. The result is that, after the backward pass, every layer has the pair $(a^{(l)},\; \partial \ell / \partial e^{(l)})$ retained — the *minimal local information* from which both per-sample scores and per-sample gradients can be reconstructed on demand. We never form the full autograd graph, and we never run backward twice.

To compute both training and target gradients in one pass, we further merge the training batch and the target batch into a single forward, with $\ell \coloneqq \sum_{i=1}^n \ell(\theta_t; z_i) + \sum_{j=1}^m \ell(\theta_t; z_j^\star)$. Because samples don't share activations, the merged backward yields per-sample backward signals for *both* sets simultaneously.

When a group can be *finalized and released* depends on partition granularity, which gives a direct connection between the statistical and the systems design:

Global Subset Update must keep $(a^{(l)}, \partial \ell / \partial e^{(l)})$ alive across <em>every</em> layer until the global subset $S_t$ is determined (long pink/orange bars); Layer-Wise Subset resolves each layer in place and releases its tensors on-the-fly, almost matching the standard-training schedule.</figcaption>
</figure>

### Efficient per-sample scoring

The score $s_i^{(l)} = \langle \hat g_\star^{(l)},\, g_i^{(l)} \rangle$ can be evaluated in several ways with strikingly different cost profiles. Let $T$ be sequence length, $n$ training samples, $m$ target samples, $N = n+m$, and $\kappa \ll d/L$ the compressed gradient dimension.

<div class="table-scroll">

| Scoring method | FLOPs per layer | Memory per layer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Direct** | $2NT(d/L) + n(d/L)$ | $(n+1)(d/L)$ |
| **GIP** (Ghost Inner Product) | $4nmT^2 \sqrt{d/L}$ | $2nmT^2$ |
| **PIP** (Per-Token Inner Product, new) | $2NT(d/L) + nT\sqrt{d/L}$ | $d/L + nT\sqrt{d/L}$ |
| **Compressed** (new) | $O(NT\kappa) + (2n+m)\kappa$ | $(n+1)\kappa$ |

</div>

**Direct** materializes per-sample gradients and pays an $O(n \cdot d/L)$ memory tax.
**GIP** ([Wang et al. 2024](https://openreview.net/forum?id=232VcN8tSx)) reorders the contraction to avoid forming $g_i^{(l)}$, but pays *quadratic* in sequence length $T$ — fatal for long-context training.
**PIP** factors the score as $s_i^{(l)} = \sum_\tau (\partial \ell / \partial e_{i,\tau}^{(l)})^\top \hat G_\star^{(l)}\, a_{i,\tau}^{(l)}$ — a matrix-vector product per token — so the cost stays *linear* in $T$ without materializing the full per-sample gradient. PIP dominates Direct whenever $T \lesssim \sqrt{d/L}$, and dominates GIP whenever $T$ is large.
**Compressed scoring** drops the $d/L$ dependence entirely by computing $\tilde g_i = \Pi^{(l)}(g_i^{(l)}) \in \mathbb{R}^\kappa$ via factorized projection ([GraSS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18976)), then approximates $s_i^{(l)} \approx \langle \tilde g_\star^{(l)},\, \tilde g_i^{(l)} \rangle$. Cheapest in every regime, at an accuracy cost that grows with the partition's granularity (see the ablations below); the main-text experiments use exact scoring.

### The full algorithm

Putting it all together, we now spell out **Layer-Wise Subset Update** — the representative case of Group-Wise Subset Update with one group per linear layer. Each layer scores its training samples, picks its top-$k$, and assembles its slice of $u_t$ in place during the backward sweep, releasing the retained pair $(a^{(\ell)}, \partial \ell / \partial e^{(\ell)})$ as soon as the layer is resolved.

<div class="algorithm">

**Algorithm.** Layer-Wise Subset Update — one optimization step.

**Input:** model $\theta_t$, training batch $B_t = \{z_i\}_{i=1}^n$, target batch $B_t^\star = \{z_j^\star\}_{j=1}^m$, subset size $k$, learning rate $\eta_t$.

**Output:** updated parameters $\theta_{t+1}$.

1. $(\ell,\; \{a^{(l)},\, e^{(l)}\}) \leftarrow \operatorname{Forward}(\theta_t,\, B_t \cup B_t^\star)$<span class="algorithm-comment">▹ merged batch, cache forward tensors</span>
2. $u_t \leftarrow 0$
3. **for** $\ell = L, \dots, 1$ **do**
4. <span class="algorithm-indent"></span>$\operatorname{Backward}(\ell,\, \theta_t^{(\ell)})$<span class="algorithm-comment">▹ per-sample $\{\partial \ell / \partial e_i^{(\ell)}\} \cup \{\partial \ell / \partial e_j^{\star(\ell)}\}$</span>
5. <span class="algorithm-indent"></span>$g_i^{(\ell)} \leftarrow \sum_{\tau} (\partial \ell / \partial e_{i,\tau}^{(\ell)}) \otimes a_{i,\tau}^{(\ell)}$<span class="algorithm-comment">▹ or PIP / compressed</span>
6. <span class="algorithm-indent"></span>$\hat g_\star^{(\ell)} \leftarrow \dfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j,\tau} (\partial \ell / \partial e_{j,\tau}^{\star(\ell)}) \otimes a_{j,\tau}^{\star(\ell)}$
7. <span class="algorithm-indent"></span>$s_i^{(\ell)} \leftarrow \langle \hat g_\star^{(\ell)},\; g_i^{(\ell)} \rangle$
8. <span class="algorithm-indent"></span>$S_{t,\ell} \leftarrow \operatorname{Top\text{-}k}(\{s_i^{(\ell)}\}_{i=1}^n,\, k)$
9. <span class="algorithm-indent"></span>$u_t^{(\ell)} \leftarrow \dfrac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in S_{t,\ell}} g_i^{(\ell)}$
10. <span class="algorithm-indent"></span>$\operatorname{Release}(a_i^{(\ell)},\; \partial \ell / \partial e_i^{(\ell)},\; a_j^{\star(\ell)},\; \partial \ell / \partial e_j^{\star(\ell)})$
11. **end for**
12. $\theta_{t+1} \leftarrow \theta_t - \eta_t u_t$

</div>

The schedule composes cleanly with **LoRA** (the outer-product factorization carries over to adapters), **MeSO** (the same compressed gradients drive both scoring and optimizer state), and **activation checkpointing** whenever the partition aligns with the checkpoint segments — Layer-Wise Subset satisfies this trivially.

## Results

We evaluate across four settings: question-answering SFT on Llama-3.2-1B, reasoning and instruction-following SFT on Qwen3-1.7B-Base, RLHF detoxification on GPT-Neo-2.7B with LoRA, and RLVR on MATH with Qwen3-1.7B-Base. Baselines are standard training (**Full-Training**) and the state-of-the-art curation methods ([**GREATS**](https://openreview.net/forum?id=232VcN8tSx) for SFT, [**IIF**](https://openreview.net/forum?id=sYK4yPDuT1) for RL — both **Global Subset** in this framework), against our **Layer-Wise Subset Update**; **Target-Only** is additionally reported on question answering. Every arm of a setting shares the hyperparameters of standard training with no per-method tuning; since the curated updates converge faster and the least regularized one can overfit within the same budget, their reported gains are conservative. All numbers are means $\pm$ standard error over 5 seeds.

Five ablations on the same four pairs sharpen the picture:

- **Random Subset controls bring no gain.** Discarding half of every batch at random — with a shared subset or one per layer — stays within a couple of points of Full-Training. The gains come from the steering by target scores, not from the smaller effective batch.
- **Partition granularity moves performance monotonically.** With exact scoring the order is Global < Block-Wise < Sublayer-Wise < Layer-Wise wherever the arms separate: on *less-mix/tydiqa* under LoRA, F1 goes $13.7 \to 33.5 \to 42.2 \to 48.3$. Refining the partition can only lower the bias terms.
- **Rescaling scores does not close the gap.** A layer-normalized Global Subset, which divides each layer's scores by their mean magnitude before summing, moves the result by a few points at most. One shared subset still cannot serve layers whose rankings disagree.
- **The ordering survives the fine-tuning method.** Under full-parameter fine-tuning Layer-Wise Subset remains the best pool-based arm on every pair ($3.5 \to 8.8 \to 32.7$ on *tydiqa*); under MeSO it stays best on the two misaligned pairs, while the compressed optimizer update costs every arm on the closed-book ones.
- **Finer partitions want exact scores.** Global Subset is insensitive to the scoring backend, whereas Layer-Wise Subset loses several points under compressed scoring on the misaligned settings — per-layer sketch noise, not bias: compressed per-layer scores correlate with exact ones at $0.89$ with a $64 \times 64$ sketch and $0.99$ at $512 \times 512$.

A case study explains *why* a shared subset falls short: per-layer score magnitudes vary by **32×** within a single transformer block (down-proj dominates Q/K/V/O and gate/up), so the summed ranking follows down-proj — Spearman $\rho \approx 0.88$ with it, but only $0.12$ and $0.11$ with Q and K — and 32 of 112 linear layers train on samples chosen for another layer. The same disagreement appears on all four pairs.

### RLHF: faster to the same reward, an order of magnitude less toxic

RLHF leaves the two-distribution setting: the PPO surrogate is now the stabilizing *training signal* for the high-variance policy gradient. We follow the standard PPO detoxification pipeline with TRL on GPT-Neo-2.7B + LoRA and exact PIP scoring, prompting from *real-toxicity-prompts*, rewarding continuations with the LFTW R4 Target model, and judging them with an independent hate-speech classifier. We compare standard PPO (Full-Training), IIF (offline selection once per PPO round), Global Subset, and Layer-Wise Subset (online selection at every mini-batch), all with *negative-score filtering*, and ablate two design choices:

- **Target source.** *Self-reference* — the rollout batch itself serves as target. *Held-out* — fresh rollouts on held-out prompts.
- **Target loss.** *Expected reward* — the REINFORCE surrogate of the reward. *Training loss* — the PPO objective itself.

The target gradient is computed once per PPO round and reused across its mini-batches: one extra backward pass per round, plus one target rollout batch for the held-out source.

Three patterns stand out:

- **The expected-reward target is far more effective than the PPO training loss**, and under it held-out rollouts converge faster than self-referencing. The reward is the quantity we actually care about; PPO's clipped loss was built to stabilize policy updates, not to point at the goal.
- **The ordering Layer-Wise > Global > IIF > standard PPO holds early in every configuration**, in both reward and toxicity. Under the reward target the curated updates reach standard PPO's final reward about five, three to four, and two to three times sooner, and Layer-Wise and Global Subset end an order of magnitude below standard PPO in toxicity, with IIF in between.
- **The reward target separates the methods at convergence; the training-loss target does not.** With the PPO loss as target the early speed-up vanishes by the end of training (insets), exactly as one expects when the target coincides with what standard PPO already optimizes.

The comparison does not hinge on the judge or the scoring backend. Re-scoring the final checkpoints with four classifiers — the paper judge, the reward model, and two English Jigsaw-trained classifiers — gives the same picture under every one of them: with the reward target, every curated update ends far below standard PPO (for the held-out reward configuration, final toxicity under the paper judge is $6.98\%$ for standard PPO against $0.68\%$ for Layer-Wise, $0.84\%$ for Global, and $1.71\%$ for IIF), and compressed scoring reproduces the ordering within one to two standard errors.

The overhead is the balance of two opposing parts. On the two small, launch-bound models the fixed cost dominates; once the weight gradient is compute-bound the saving pays for it, and on Llama-3.2-3B the curated step is $1$–$2\%$ *faster* than Full-Training. <div class="takeaway">

**Takeaway.** The leverage is the *primal–dual* view of **data selection ↔ data regularization**. Jumping between the two puts existing methods on a single bias–variance spectrum, says which point on it should win from the mismatch and the target batch size, and suggests methods the primal view never would — with concrete surprises along the way, such as the 32× per-layer score-magnitude gap that lets Layer-Wise beat Global Subset.
