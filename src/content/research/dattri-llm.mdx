**TL;DR.** **dattri-LLM** makes gradient-based training data attribution (TDA) practical at LLM scale. The core idea is that a per-example weight gradient of a linear layer is *exactly* $\sum_t a_t \otimes g_t$, so the library keeps gradients in a compact **factorized** $(a, g)$ form or **materializes** them, and a **FLOP-aware cost model** picks the cheaper route per layer and per operation — no extra approximation. Around that core sit **non-invasive gradient capture** (wrap any `backward()`-based loop — HuggingFace `Trainer`, TRL, OLMo, under DDP or FSDP — in `with hm.collect():`) and **two extension points** (`Attributor` for new methods, `HookManagerCallback` for applications that act during training). On the same hardware and workload it averages $3.2\times$ the throughput of the fastest competing library, scales GradDot, K-FAC, and EK-FAC to $110$B-parameter models on four H200s, and traces the fidelity–cost Pareto frontier against leave-one-out retraining.

Most scalable TDA methods — TracIn, TRAK, EK-FAC influence, LESS, AdamW-influence — share the same building blocks: obtain per-example gradients, optionally project or precondition them, and take inner products. At LLM scale, each block is a systems problem. A single per-example gradient has billions of entries; existing libraries require editing the training loop or adopting a specific trainer; and adding a new method or a training-time application means re-implementing gradient capture from scratch. [dattri](/research/dattri/) solved this for single-GPU, sub-billion-parameter models. **dattri-LLM** is its successor for models with tens to hundreds of billions of parameters across multiple GPUs.

## Why dattri-LLM

- **[Efficiency without approximation](#efficiency-two-exact-gradient-representations--flop-aware-routing).** Factorized and materialized gradients are *both exact*; the library chooses between them per layer and operation using a FLOP cost model. Averaged over methods and projection regimes, this yields $3.2\times$ the throughput of the fastest competing library at every configuration.
- **[Compatibility with the loop you already have](#compatibility-non-invasive-capture).** Autograd hooks capture per-example gradients from any loop that calls `backward()`. Unmodified HuggingFace `Trainer`, TRL, and OLMo-core pipelines are instrumented by wrapping `trainer.train()` in a context manager — under DDP and FSDP too.
- **[Extensibility at two seams](#extensibility-two-extension-points).** Subclass `Attributor` to add an attribution method on top of a shared gradient stream and routed operations. Subclass `HookManagerCallback` to inspect or *modify* training state at capture time — e.g., online data selection in three lines.
- **[Scales where others stop](#scaling-to-110b-parameters).** All three of GradDot, K-FAC, and EK-FAC run through $110$B parameters on four H200s; at $3$B, curvature-based methods use $14$–$21\%$ of the GPU memory of Kronfluence and Bergson.
- **[Faithful, not just fast](#fidelity-vs-cost).** Against trajectory-specific leave-one-out retraining (TSLOO) on GPT-2, Qwen2.5-1.5B, and OLMo-3-7B, dattri-LLM's configurations form the empirical Pareto frontier in two of three settings; full AdamW-influence reaches $0.845$ Spearman on GPT-2 vs. MAGIC's $0.833$, in less time.

## Design

### Efficiency: two exact gradient representations + FLOP-aware routing

Consider a linear layer $z = Wa$ with $W \in \mathbb{R}^{N_o \times N_i}$, per-example input $a \in \mathbb{R}^{N_i \times T}$ over $T$ token positions, and output gradient $g = \mathrm{D}z \in \mathbb{R}^{N_o \times T}$. By the chain rule the weight gradient is $\mathrm{D}W = g\,a^\top$, or in flattened form

$$
\gamma := \operatorname{vec}(\mathrm{D}W) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} a_t \otimes g_t .
$$

The dense matrix $\mathrm{D}W$ is the **materialized** representation; the pair $(a, g)$ is the **factorized** one. Both are exact — the factors recover the gradient. The factorized form stores $T(N_i + N_o)$ values versus $N_i N_o$ for the materialized form, so it wins for *wide layers and short sequences*. Convolutional and embedding layers admit analogous factorizations.

The key payoff is that attribution never needs to materialize if it doesn't want to. For two samples with $T_i$ and $T_j$ tokens, the Kronecker inner-product identity gives the exact gradient inner product straight from the factors:

$$
\left\langle \gamma^{(i)}, \gamma^{(j)} \right\rangle
= \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \sum_{s=1}^{T_j}
\left\langle a_t^{(i)}, a_s^{(j)} \right\rangle
\left\langle g_t^{(i)}, g_s^{(j)} \right\rangle .
$$

The same factorized arithmetic supports K-FAC / EK-FAC preconditioning, and because the token axis is retained, scores decompose to *token granularity* for free.

**Neither route is uniformly cheaper.** For a cross-Gram matrix between batches of $B_1$ and $B_2$ examples of length $T$, the factorized route costs $O\!\left(B_1 B_2 T^2 (N_i + N_o)\right)$ — quadratic in sequence length — while materializing costs $O\!\left((B_1 + B_2)\, T N_i N_o + B_1 B_2 N_i N_o\right)$ — a construction cost amortized over subsequent dense inner products. Short sequences and wide layers favor factors; long sequences and large comparison batches favor materialization. Projection changes the balance again by replacing $N_i, N_o$ with the projected dimensions $k_a, k_g$. dattri-LLM estimates the FLOP cost of each route *per layer and per operation* and takes the cheaper one.

<div class="remark">

**Routing ablation.** On Pythia-410M with full-dimensional GradDot and sequence lengths from $32$ to $2048$, adaptive routing stays within $2.8\%$ of the faster fixed route at every length — while always-materialized runs take $16$–$50\%$ longer at $T \le 512$ and always-factorized takes $17\%$ longer at $T = 2048$. At $T = 32$, always-materialized also uses $4.8\times$ the peak memory of adaptive routing.

</div>

**Postprocessing.** Two optional transforms sit before scoring. *Dimension reduction* can act on the factors — $\tilde a_t = P_a a_t$, $\tilde g_t = P_g g_t$, so $\sum_t \tilde a_t \otimes \tilde g_t = (P_a \otimes P_g)\gamma$ preserves the factorized structure in the projected space (the trick behind LoGRA and [GraSS](/research/GraSS/)) — or on the materialized gradient via a single dense projection, as in TRAK. Gaussian, Rademacher, sparse JL, GraSS's sparsified projection, and random coordinate masks are all supported. *Optimizer preconditioning* turns a per-example gradient into the update direction the training optimizer would actually take, using its live first- and second-moment state — enabling optimizer-aware methods such as LESS and [AdamW-influence](/research/Faithful-Trajectory-Attribution/).

### Compatibility: non-invasive capture

`HookManager` registers autograd hooks on selected layers and captures the two factors $(a, g)$ during every backward pass that fires inside a `with` block. Nothing about the loop, optimizer, or trainer changes:

```python
hm = HookManager(model)
with hm.collect():
    trainer.train()        # unmodified HuggingFace Trainer / TRL / OLMo-core
```

This works uniformly across pretraining, supervised fine-tuning, and RL loops, and under DDP and FSDP. Captured gradients can be cached to disk and reused across queries and attribution methods, so the training-side cost is paid once.

### Extensibility: two extension points

- **`Attributor`** — the seam for *new methods*. `GradientStreamer` provides a uniform per-example gradient stream; the gradient tier provides routed inner products, norms, projections, and Kronecker-factored statistics. A new method is one subclass consuming these modules. The built-in TracIn, K-FAC, EK-FAC, and data-value-embedding attributors are implemented through exactly this interface.
- **`HookManagerCallback`** — the seam for *training-time applications*. A callback receives capture events at each step and may inspect or modify training state. Built-ins include an offloading callback that persists gradients to disk and a data-selection callback that scores the current batch against a target gradient and removes low-scoring examples' contributions from the parameter gradients before the optimizer step.

## Results

### Scaling to 110B parameters

Four libraries — dattri-LLM, [LogIX](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13954), [Bergson](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.11660), and [Kronfluence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03296) — on GradDot, K-FAC, and EK-FAC over the Qwen family from $0.5$B to $110$B, $512$-token WikiText-103 sequences in bf16, four H200s ($141$GB each), factor projection $k_a = k_g = 64$ where supported, and the largest per-device batch that fits.

### Online data selection in three lines

The extensibility claim is tested by porting the attribution-guided data selection of [Dr. Post-Training](/research/Dr-Post-Training/) into a *minimal* fine-tuning loop. At each step, GradDot scores between the current batch and a held-out target set are computed, and the bottom half of the batch is dropped from the update.

```python
from dattri_llm import DataSelectionCallback, HookManager

# 1. Define the selection policy
select = DataSelectionCallback(
    model, target="val_loader",
    val_loader=target_loader, val_loss_fn=loss_fn,
    selection_kwargs={"threshold": 0.5, "threshold_mode": "bottom_fraction"})
# 2. Attach it to the capture hook
hm = HookManager(model, callbacks=[select])
# 3. Train as usual
with hm.collect():
    for batch in train_loader:
        loss_fn(model, batch).backward()
        optimizer.step(); optimizer.zero_grad()
```

Between the backward pass and the optimizer step, the callback scores the batch and removes dropped samples' contributions from the parameter gradients. LoRA fine-tuning differs only by a layer selector that restricts hooks to the adapters.

Layer-wise selection reaches a final SAMSum perplexity of $5.11$ vs. $6.31$ for full training under full-parameter fine-tuning, and $4.87$ vs. $6.31$ under LoRA — reproducing the Dr. Post-Training finding without its dedicated training pipeline. Per-step runtime is $89$ ms for standard training, $146$ ms with dattri-LLM selection, and $154$ ms for the original dedicated implementation: the library version is about $5\%$ *faster*, because it reuses the training backward pass rather than running an extra forward pass to obtain training gradients.

<div class="takeaway">

**Takeaway.** At LLM scale, data attribution is a systems problem before it is a statistics problem. dattri-LLM organizes the stack at the level every gradient-based method shares — per-example gradients, projection or preconditioning, pairwise scoring — and makes one decision well: *which exact representation of the gradient to hold, per layer and per operation*. That decision, plus capture that attaches to the training loop you already have and seams for new methods and training-time applications, is what turns $3.2\times$ average throughput, $110$B-parameter reach, and a Pareto-frontier fidelity profile into the same library.

</div>
